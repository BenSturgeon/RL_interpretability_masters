\chapter{Introduction}\label{ch:introduction}

Understanding how deep learning models function is currently an unsolved problem in the field of machine learning. This state of affairs ensures that highly reliable, controllable and understandable models remain out of reach. This is particularly the case in the field of reinforcement learning (RL), which has been somewhat neglected by the techniques of Mechanistic Interpretability (MI) whose focus has largely been on Large Language Models (LLMs)~\cite{elhage2021mathematical}. With techniques from RL being applied to frontier models to a greater extent, it appears that a gap exists in the application of MI techniques to RL models.

Existing techniques are being used to better understand RL models~\citep{atrey_2020_exploratory}, but many techniques developed for MI elsewhere have not yet seen much application in RL (\citep{hilton_2020_understanding} being a notable exception). RL poses natural difficulties for interpretation due to the nature of RL outputs as actions or sequences of actions rather than as words or images. This adds to the challenge of extracting meaningful insights from the earlier layers of the model. The unpredictable nature of RL training further adds to these challenges, as variability in learning outcomes makes it difficult to determine the genuine nature of what the model learns.

Furthermore, given the high likelihood that RL systems are deployed in the operation of critical infrastructure such as data centres or nuclear fusion reactors~\cite{Degrave_2022_Nature_Fusion}, there is significant urgency to guarantee high standards of operational safety and to show that policies function predictably within specified operational bounds, so that we avoid catastrophic failures in these systems~\citep{Amodei_2016_AISafety}.

There is an opportunity to explore novel techniques for interpreting other categories of AI systems with RL, as RL environments usually contain relatively simple goals and easily determined failure modes that allow for testing whether changes evoke a desired behaviour. For example, the approach of steering Large Language Models (LLMs) using activation steering in~\cite{turner_2024_activation} is inspired by work to understand and control a maze-solving agent in the Procgen Maze environment~\cite{mini_2023_understanding}.

The investigation seeks to build on the work of~\cite{mini_2023_understanding}, who identify key parts of the model that can be used to control model behaviour. This investigation's contribution is to extend their work to an environment that involves multiple competing entities that an agent needs to reach in sequence. To this end, the Procgen Heist environment is chosen as the target of this research. This is because the Procgen environment requires the agent to navigate through three locks of different colours, each requiring a corresponding key before reaching a gem at the end. This means that the agent has to learn robust mechanisms for navigating to each entity in the environment~\citep{cobbe_2020_leveraging}. By observing how the model learns to approach multi-objective maze-solving, this work aims to understand how neural networks represent and pursue multiple sequential goals.

A significant challenge in understanding the internal mechanisms of complex neural networks, particularly those operating in multifaceted environments like Procgen Heist, is the prevalence of polysemanticity~\cite{elhage2022superposition}. This phenomenon, where individual neurons respond to multiple distinct features, can make it challenging to draw strong conclusions about how a model works. To address this, Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) can be employed. SAEs are a type of unsupervised learning technique that can be used to break down the most critical functions of a model into a more interpretable set of sparse latents, often by training an overcomplete basis on the original activations of a given layer of the network~\cite{bricken2023monosemanticity}. In this study, we apply this approach to the convolutional neural network (CNN) layers to disentangle model functionality. Our methodology for adapting SAEs to CNNs builds upon recent work by~\cite{gorton_2024_missing}, and we describe it in later sections.



\section{Background and Motivation}\label{sec:background}

RL is a field of machine learning that has the unique promise of delivering superhuman performance in a wide range of environments~\cite{Silver2016}. However, the black box nature of these models makes it difficult to understand how they achieve these results. In recent years, RL sees a surge in interest from the ML community, due to its application in LLMs and other frontier models~\cite{ouyang2022training, bai2022training}. This leads to significant advances in performance in these models, but also presents the potential for these highly capable systems to manifest unexpected behaviour that is unique to the RL setting~\citep{krakovna2020faulty}.

Machine learning as a field is composed primarily of three different approaches to training models. These are supervised learning, unsupervised learning, and RL. Supervised learning uses explicitly labelled data to allow models to learn the underlying patterns that connect the input to the specific label. Unsupervised learning uses no labels and simply has the model learn structures by identifying underlying patterns in the data.  

RL differs in that it focuses on the idea of an environment that is the source of truth for the agent. By providing actions to the environment based on its policy, the agent receives new information in the form of observations from the environment, as well as reward information that provides a signal about whether it achieves the goals that the designers of the model and environment intend~\cite{sutton2018reinforcement}. This is typically done by using the reward as a key variable in the loss function, with higher rewards being linked to lower loss. Though for some approaches such as temporal difference learning, the loss is reduced by minimising the difference between the predicted and actual value of the next state~\cite{sutton2018reinforcement}.

This means that with relatively low amounts of human effort in shaping environment behaviour, agents trained with RL can achieve performance that far surpasses human capabilities in a domain, as demonstrated by systems such as AlphaGo~\citep{Silver2016}. In that case, the agent trains through self-play, playing against a similarly skilled version of itself, and learns to improve on its mistakes based on whether the moves it makes in a game lead to victory or defeat.

Modern RL often employs deep neural networks in order to achieve some of its most remarkable results, but this leads to the issue of the lack of interpretability into how those results are achieved due to the black box nature of modern neural networks.

The field of MI seeks to help decode the black boxes of neural networks so that these models can be used with greater confidence and reliability, and to allow for proactive identification of issues in models' behaviour before critical issues arise~\cite{olah2018building}. 

MI as a field aims to answer the question of how neural networks actually function at a very granular level. In the ideal case it makes it possible to reverse engineer a given neural network into a human-readable algorithm that can be used to generate the same outputs as the network, through a complete knowledge of how its parts work. Having this understanding provides a broad set of tools for helping to make neural networks safer and more reliable.

MI broadly refers to a number of approaches that can be used to gain insight into neural network operations, from visualising neurons and learned filters in CNNs, to the training of specialised neural networks that help better understand the operations of a particular part of a network. 

The ultimate goal of MI is to find ways to ensure the safety of AI systems, and this is particularly critical with frontier agentic AI that can perform long-horizon tasks that may involve actions that are highly impactful to humans. It is critical to understand and prevent unwanted intent from developing in such systems, and to do this it is necessary to understand what goals the system may have. 

Early in the field's development, researchers were unsure whether neural networks achieved their results through the construction of internal models in such a way that it was coherent to discuss it could have `intent' or `goals'. However, recent work provides compelling evidence that neural networks can indeed learn meaningful internal models and conceptual abstractions from data. For instance, studies on language models trained only on the moves made in a game such as Othello, with no explanation of the rules, reveal that these models spontaneously develop internal representations of the game state without explicit supervision of the rules~\citep{li2022emergent}. 

Other research shows that such models naturally learn geographic mappings of the world related to locations that can be retrieved directly from the activations of the network, effectively building simplified world models~\citep{gurnee2023language}. Similarly, analyses of agents trained on complex games like chess demonstrate that these systems acquire human-understandable strategic concepts, such as piece values or tactical motifs, purely from self-play~\citep{mcgrathAcquisitionChessKnowledge2022}. These findings suggest that the internal workings of these models are richer than previously thought and lend support to the idea that they plausibly learn to represent goals that can be directly extracted from the model and understood.

It therefore seems plausible that something as fundamental as goals is likely to share some properties in the way it is encoded across models. With that hope in mind, this work seeks to reveal some of these properties to inform investigations across future models.

However, not all models that seek to target goals are good candidates for this research, as it may be the case that the task is simple enough to solve without the need to develop a sufficiently complex mechanism to represent abstract concepts like goals. The Procgen environments stand out as good environments for this reason, as they procedurally generate environments for the agent to solve. This means that the agent cannot simply overfit to the data, but has to learn general patterns in order to score well on the environment, which necessitates developing a complex representation of goals within the
 model.

The choice to use Procgen Heist in particular stemmed from the desire to explore how multiple goals might be represented in the model, as significant work has already shown the presence of `cheese representations' with the Procgen Maze environment\citep{mini_2023_understanding}. Because advanced AI agents would be operating in environments with multiple goals, the question of whether there was a substantial difference in the representation of these goals is an essential one.

However, in making the environment more complex the model too becomes more complex, and the problem of polysemantic neurons becomes a significant hurdle to understanding the model's operations. Specifically, whether the model's representations of goals in the base model represent a `true' representation or simply result from polysemanticity in the network. 

Polysemanticity refers to the case of a single neuron having multiple coherent feature representations encoded simultaneously, due to it needing to encode many such features but not having sufficient neurons to represent them all. In such cases the model will compress multiple features into a single neuron, typically encoding features that are unlikely to activate strongly simultaneously in practice to avoid causing harmful interference. 

This leads to the use of Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) to confirm whether neurons in the network naturally tend to represent the different entities using different neurons, or whether there are single `current goal' channels in the network that naturally come to exist. 

An SAE is a model trained with an unsupervised approach to optimally reconstruct the outputs of a given layer in the network. Typically it is trained with a loss function that minimises the reconstruction loss and the L1 norm of the network, which means that it produces the smallest possible activations. Typically the SAE has a hidden layer between an encoder and decoder which are the primary parameters of the network, and the size of the hidden layer is normally based on the size of the original layer multiplied by some expansion factor. This means that the SAE learns an overcomplete basis of the original layer, which allows for the separation of polysemantic features into separate neurons in the network, which can then be analysed in isolation~\cite{bricken2023monosemanticity}.

Being able to understand how these models work is a key step towards building more reliable, controllable and understandable models. Specifically, there is interest in understanding how these models represent and pursue goals in complex, multi-objective environments that are a step beyond simple toy environments. Goals, specifically, are something that advanced AI agents share with even simple models, and so greater knowledge of the underlying principles behind this universal concept may provide fundamental insights that generalise beyond toy environments.



\section{Problem Statement}\label{sec:problem}
As AI systems become more complex and are deployed more widely, it becomes increasingly necessary to provide safety guarantees and explanations behind critical trade-offs that RL policies are making so that these systems can be deployed with confidence that they will work as expected~\citep{shakya_2023_reinforcement}.

If we are unable to understand how goals specifically are represented and pursued within RL models, we risk deploying systems that may pursue unintended or misaligned objectives, leading to unpredictable or harmful outcomes, especially in complex, multi-step tasks. This challenge is exacerbated by the inherent opacity of deep neural networks, where phenomena such as polysemanticity, a key focus of this investigation, can obscure the goal-related features we wish to isolate and understand. Without a clear understanding of these internal goal representations, ensuring agent alignment with intended behaviors, debugging undesirable policies, and building reliable autonomous systems remains a formidable task. The lack of such understanding in the context of multi-objective sequential decision-making, as typified by environments like Procgen Heist, forms the central problem this thesis aims to address. Addressing this problem necessitates a direct inquiry into the nature of these representations and the methods to uncover them, leading to the following research objectives.



\section{Research Objectives}\label{sec:objectives}
This research seeks to demonstrate that it is possible to determine the key functional components in a maze-solving agent that are responsible for determining the agent's goal. To this end, our specific objectives are to:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Determine what parts of the model are uniquely responsible for abstract goal formulation and how goals are represented in the model weights. This includes investigating whether the model captures goals with singular representations within channels, or if there are general \texttt{current goal} or entity-specific steering channels in the network.
    \item Assess the utility of Sparse AutoEncoders (SAEs) in disentangling polysemantic activations to better determine the role of specific, interpretable features in goal representation.
    \item Investigate the mechanisms by which certain SAE channels, including those with low baseline activity (so-called \texttt{dead} channels), can nonetheless influence or steer the network's behavior when subject to interventions.
    \item Explore the factors contributing to observed differences in success rates when attempting to steer the agent's behavior away from various entities or objectives within its environment.
    \item Evaluate how effectively SAEs preserve the agent's task performance while modifying the original model's activations, thereby aiming to ensure that the features learned by the SAEs are relevant to the agent's operational policy.
    \item Identify whether different neural network layers, either in the base model or in the derived SAEs, exhibit specialized roles in the processing of goal-related information or in their susceptibility to steering interventions.
\end{itemize}
Through these objectives, particularly by leveraging SAEs, we aim to provide clearer evidence of how the model learns, represents, and pursues sequential goals in an environment such as Procgen Heist.



\section{Research Questions}\label{sec:questions}
This research seeks to answer the following key questions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item How are goals represented in the model weights?
    \item Can we use Sparse AutoEncoders (SAEs) to better determine the role of specific features?
    \item Why do dead channels seem able to still steer the network?
    \item Do specific channels target specific entities, or are there general goal steering channels in the network?
    \item Why do we observe different rates of success when steering the agent away from different entities?
    \item Given that SAEs modify the original model\'s activations, how effectively do they preserve the agent\'s task performance, ensuring that their learned features are relevant to the agent\'s behaviour?
    \item Does the research indicate that different neural network layers (in the base model or SAEs) play specialized roles in how the agent processes goal information or becomes steerable?
\end{enumerate}



\section{Key Hypotheses}\label{sec:hypotheses}
Based on the preceding research questions, this study investigates the following key hypotheses:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item We hypothesize that the model predominantly learns to represent specific goals (like individual keys or the gem) via dedicated channels, rather than relying solely on generalized `current target' signals or highly distributed, non-specific encodings.
    \item We hypothesize that Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) deconstruct complex, polysemantic activations within the agent's layers into a more disentangled and interpretable set of features, thereby clarifying the specific roles of underlying neural computations related to goals.
    \item We hypothesize that the observed influence of apparently `dead' or low-activation SAE channels during steering interventions arises from the residual impact of their learned decoder weights.
    \item We hypothesize that variations in the success rates of steering the agent away from different entities are linked to the amount of time that the model spends in direct observation of those entities.
    \item We hypothesize that SAEs can be trained to reconstruct the original model's activations with sufficient fidelity to preserve the agent's task performance, indicating that the features learned by the SAE are indeed relevant to the agent's operational policy.
    \item We hypothesize that distinct layers within the Procgen agent's network (and by extension, the SAEs trained on them) play specialized roles in processing goal-related information, potentially with earlier layers focusing on perceptual parsing of the location of entities and the structure of the maze, while later layers encode more abstract information such as which entity to pursue and plans for how to reach it.
\end{enumerate}



\section{Scope and Limitations}\label{sec:scope}
This work is limited by the scope of the Procgen Heist environment, which is a simplified environment that may not fully capture the complexity of real-world decision-making in sequential, multi-objective tasks. It is also limited by the use of a single model architecture and a single environment. 

Furthermore, the reliance on Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) as the primary interpretability tool introduces its own set of limitations. The features discovered by SAEs can be sensitive to hyperparameter choices, such as the sparsity coefficient and dictionary size, which can influence the nature of the learned representations. This can create a gap between the features the SAE learns and the true underlying computational nature of the network. Additionally, the nature of the RL environment itself poses a challenge; much of the model's complexity is likely encoded in functionally critical but semantically ``illegible'' factors, such as subtle patterns for navigating obstacles. While SAEs may successfully isolate high-level goal features, they may struggle to render these low-level navigational strategies intelligible. Consequently, interpreting the full suite of discovered features remains a significant challenge, and SAEs may not capture all of the original model's computational strategies.

While the environment is a good fit for this research, its complexity means that the construction of experiments is quite time-consuming, which is the reason why only this environment is used.

There are also a number of cutting-edge MI techniques that become available over the course of this research, such as the use of bilinear activation functions to do weight-based interpretability, and parameter-based decomposition, which allows for the decomposition of a given channel into a sum of other activations, which can then be individually analysed and understood. Due to the time constraints of the research, these are not explored.



\section{Significance of the Study}\label{sec:significance}
This research contributes to the field of MI by demonstrating the application of Sparse Autoencoders, a technique largely developed for language models, to the distinct challenges of reinforcement learning agents. Understanding goal representation is fundamental to ensuring agent alignment, especially as RL is increasingly used in safety-critical and frontier AI systems. By providing a pathway for disentangling and interpreting how an RL agent represents its objectives, this work offers new techniques and insights for decoding the internal mechanisms of these models.

Furthermore, the study's findings have broader implications for how different entities within an environment are captured by CNN models. The investigation into phenomena like `dead channel' steering contributes to our fundamental knowledge of both neural network function and the properties of our interpretability tools. Some of the experimental frameworks used provide a replicable template for future studies seeking to causally probe goal-directed behaviours in neural networks. Ultimately, such insights yield new research directions on how environmental factors shape learning dynamics and help inform the development of more robust and reliable AI systems.
