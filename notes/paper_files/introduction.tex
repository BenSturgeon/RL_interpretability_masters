\chapter{Introduction}\label{ch:introduction}

Reinforcement learning agents are increasingly deployed in critical infrastructure, from data centres~\citep{luo_2022_controlling} to electrical grids~\citep{nakabi_2021_deep} and nuclear fusion reactors~\citep{Degrave_2022_Nature_Fusion}. Yet how these agents internally represent and pursue goals remains poorly understood. This opacity poses significant risks: without understanding goal representations, we cannot ensure alignment, debug failures, or build reliable autonomous systems~\citep{Amodei_2016_AISafety}.

While mechanistic interpretability (MI) has made substantial progress in understanding Large Language Models~\citep{elhage2021mathematical, wang_2022_interpretability, olsson_2022_incontext, mcdougall_2023_copy}, RL agents have received comparatively little attention, with notable exceptions~\citep{hilton_2020_understanding, atrey_2020_exploratory}. This gap is significant: RL outputs are action sequences rather than words or images, making interpretation inherently more challenging since actions are often only meaningful over broader sequences. Yet RL environments offer a unique opportunity for MI research, as they contain well-defined goals and clear failure modes that allow direct testing of mechanistic hypotheses. Indeed, recent work on steering LLMs~\citep{turner_2024_activation} was directly inspired by interpretability work on maze-solving agents~\citep{mini_2023_understanding}.

This thesis investigates how neural networks represent and pursue multiple sequential goals in RL agents. Building on~\citet{mini_2023_understanding}, which identified channels controlling goal pursuit in single-objective mazes, we extend this analysis to the Procgen Heist environment where agents must collect up to three keys in sequence before reaching a gem~\citep{cobbe_2020_leveraging}. A key challenge in this investigation is polysemanticity~\citep{elhage2022superposition}, the phenomenon whereby individual neurons respond to multiple distinct features, which can obscure goal representations and complicate interpretation.

\section{Background and Motivation}\label{sec:background}

RL is a field of machine learning that has the unique promise of delivering superhuman performance in a wide range of environments~\citep{silver_2016_mastering}. However, the ``black box'' nature of these models makes it difficult to understand how they achieve these results.

RL agents learn through interaction with an environment, receiving observations and rewards that guide policy improvement~\citep{sutton2018reinforcement}. The field of MI seeks to decode the black boxes of neural networks so that these models can be used with greater confidence and reliability~\citep{olah2018building}. Recent work provides compelling evidence that neural networks can learn meaningful internal models and conceptual abstractions from data~\citep{li2022emergent}, suggesting that understanding goal representations in one model may inform investigations of others. That said, not all goal-seeking models are suitable for this research; some tasks are simple enough to solve without developing complex mechanisms to represent abstract goals. The Procgen environments stand out as good environments for this reason, as they constitute procedurally generated environments for the agent to solve. This means that the agent cannot simply overfit to the data, but has to learn general patterns in order to score well on the environment, which necessitates developing a complex representation of goals within the model.

We chose Procgen Heist in particular to explore how multiple goals might be represented in the model, as previous work has already shown the presence of `cheese representations' with the Procgen Maze environment~\citep{mini_2023_understanding}. Because advanced AI agents would be operating in environments with multiple goals, the question of whether there was a substantial difference in the representation of these goals is an essential one.

As the environment becomes more complex, so does the model, and polysemanticity becomes a significant hurdle to understanding the model's operations. Specifically, it becomes difficult to determine whether the model's representations of goals reflect a `true' representation or simply result from polysemanticity or pattern memorisation in the network. This led to the use of Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs), as well as a variety of fundamental interpretability approaches, which are discussed more in Section~\ref{sec:interpretability}, to confirm whether channels in the network naturally tend to represent the different entities using dedicated channels, or whether there are single `current goal' channels in the network that are re-used across different entities.



\section{Problem Statement}\label{sec:problem}
How do RL agents internally represent and select between multiple sequential goals? Without understanding this, we cannot verify that agents pursue intended objectives, diagnose failures when they occur, or predict behaviour in novel situations. This problem is particularly acute in multi-objective environments where the same visual features (keys, doors, targets) must be processed differently depending on task progress. The inherent opacity of deep neural networks, compounded by polysemanticity, makes isolating goal-related representations challenging. This thesis addresses this gap by investigating goal representation mechanisms in the Procgen Heist environment, where agents must track and switch between multiple sequential objectives.

\section{Research Questions}\label{sec:questions}
This research seeks to determine the key functional components in a maze-solving agent that are responsible for determining the agent's goal, and to provide clearer evidence of how the network learns, represents, and pursues sequential goals. To this end, we investigate the following key questions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item[\textbf{RQ1:}] How are the different goals encoded in the network?
    \item[\textbf{RQ2:}] What mechanism does the model use to determine which entity to target?
    \item[\textbf{RQ3:}] Can we use SAEs to better determine the role of specific features?
    \item[\textbf{RQ4:}] Do specific channels target specific entities, or are there general goal steering channels in the network?
    \item[\textbf{RQ5:}] Why do we observe different rates of success when steering the agent away from different entities using activation patching?
    \item[\textbf{RQ6:}] Do different neural network layers play specialised roles in how the agent processes goal information or becomes steerable?
\end{enumerate}



\section{Key Hypotheses}\label{sec:hypotheses}
Based on the preceding research questions, this study aims to test the following key hypotheses:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item[\textbf{H1:}] We hypothesise that the model predominantly learns to represent specific goals via dedicated channels, rather than relying solely on generalised `current target' signals or highly distributed, non-specific encodings~\citep{bau_2017_network, cammarata2020curve}.
    \item[\textbf{H2:}] We hypothesise that Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) deconstruct complex, polysemantic activations within the agent's layers into a more disentangled and interpretable set of features, thereby clarifying the specific roles of channels into specific circuits related to goals~\citep{bricken2023monosemanticity}.
    \item[\textbf{H3:}] We hypothesise that the model has a strong sequential preference structure that is encoded deeply in the weights~\citep{langosco2022goal}.
    \item[\textbf{H4:}] We hypothesise that SAEs can be trained to reconstruct the original model's activations with sufficient fidelity to preserve the agent's task performance, indicating that the features learned by the SAE are indeed relevant to the agent's operational policy~\citep{templeton_scaling_2024, bricken2023monosemanticity}.
    \item[\textbf{H5:}] We hypothesise that distinct layers within the Procgen agent's network play specialised roles in processing goal-related information, potentially with earlier layers focusing on perceptual parsing of the location of entities and the structure of the maze, while intermediate layers encode more abstract information such as which entity to pursue and while later layers produce plans for how to reach it~\citep{zeiler_2013_visualizing, bau_2017_network, trim_2024_mechanistic}.
\end{enumerate}


\section{Scope and Limitations}\label{sec:scope}
We focus on a single environment (Heist) to perform deep mechanistic analysis rather than a broad empirical analysis. This approach follows established precedent in RL interpretability research~\citep{mini_2023_understanding,hilton_2020_understanding, trim_2024_mechanistic}, where thorough investigation of specific mechanisms within one environment has proven more valuable than superficial analysis across many domains. The mechanisms we discover raise questions worthy of investigating in other multi-objective RL domains, though we cannot assume they generalise beyond this specific setting.

We trained a custom model from scratch rather than using a typical variant for this environment, using the architecture described in~\citet{hilton_2020_understanding}. This simplified model allowed for analysis of a narrower surface area, by using fewer layers and removing skip connections that otherwise confound interpretation.

Additionally, the nature of the RL environment itself poses a challenge; much of the model's complexity is likely encoded in functionally critical but semantically uninterpretable features, such as subtle patterns for navigating obstacles, and is further complicated by the fact that the network has a split policy and value head, meaning some of the work done in the later layers is not entirely to do with actions but also in calculating the value of the current state, the impact of which is challenging to measure. While SAEs and probes may successfully isolate high-level goal features, they may struggle to render these low-level navigational strategies intelligible. Consequently, interpreting the full suite of discovered features remains a significant challenge, as our tools may not capture all of the original model's computational strategies.


\section{Significance of the Study}\label{sec:significance}
This work applies SAEs to an RL environment, which remains relatively uncommon. A great deal of recent MI work has been applied to LLMs rather than RL agents, meaning that the space of tools and approaches is relatively small compared to other types of neural networks. Our hope is that in the extensive application of these tools to this network we can help to mature the discourse on approaching these sorts of problem and clarify the strengths and weaknesses of these tools in this domain. 

We discover that the network uses spatial gating through the use of negative and positive activations in the CNN layers, where negative activations mark areas of the maze that the agent still needs to go to, and which are filled in with positive activations as entities in those regions are collected. This finding has implications for how different entities within an environment are captured by a model trained with RL. Some of the experimental frameworks used provide a replicable template for future studies seeking to causally understand goal-directed behaviours in neural networks.
