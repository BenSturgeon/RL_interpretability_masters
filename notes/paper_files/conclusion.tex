\section{Summary of the Research}
\label{sec:research_summary}
% Brief recap of the entire thesis
% Main problem addressed and approach taken
This thesis investigated the internal representations and goal-directed behavior of deep reinforcement learning agents, specifically focusing on a model trained on the multi-objective Procgen Heist environment. The research employed mechanistic interpretability techniques, including the training and analysis of Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) on the agent's convolutional layers, feature visualization, activation steering, and channel ablation studies. The primary aim was to understand how such agents represent complex, sequential goals and how tools like SAEs can assist in disentangling and interpreting these representations. Building on previous work that demonstrated control of RL agents via activation manipulation, this research extended these methods to a more complex setting and explored the utility of SAEs in revealing finer-grained details of neural computation within RL agents.

\section{Key Findings and Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}
% Most important results and their significance
% Original contributions to the field

    The principal findings and contributions of this research are as follows:

    % From Paper 1 Conclusion
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Successfully trained Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) on the CNN layers of the Heist model, recovering >98\% of the original task performance.
        \item Demonstrated that precise steering of the agent towards different entities could be achieved by intervening on single channels in multiple layers (`conv3a`, `conv4a`) of both the base model and SAE-enhanced models.
        \item Revealed that while some channels appeared to track specific entities and also affect steering, other SAE channels were highly efficient at tracking/navigation during ablations but could not unilaterally modify the agent's trajectory, indicating specialized roles within the SAE.
        \item Uncovered the surprising property that a large number of "dead channels" in the SAEs (channels with no activation during normal operation) could nonetheless be used to successfully steer the agent. This was explained as an artifact of the SAE's decoder architecture retaining learned weights.
        \item Found that the model was still capable of navigating to different keys even when only a single SAE channel was active, highlighting a high degree of functional compression.
        \item Discovered that certain critical steering channels within the SAEs did not simply represent single entities, but rather encoded different entities at different activation strengths, revealing a sophisticated mechanism for multiplexing information within a single channel.
    \end{itemize}

    % From Paper 2 (implicit in its summary/contributions)
    Additional key insights include:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Confirmation that steering vectors can be a valuable tool for identifying goal-tracking channels and for applying targeted control, even in more complex, multi-objective environments like Procgen Heist.
        \item Observation that later convolutional layers in the base model (e.g., layer 8 in the simplified architecture) tend to exhibit more specialized behavior related to goal representation compared to earlier, more polysemantic layers.
        \item Reinforcement of the idea that simplified model architectures can facilitate interpretability in RL agents, as suggested by \cite{hilton_understanding_2020}.
    \end{itemize}

\section{Implications for Theory and Practice}
\label{sec:impl_theory_practice}
% How your work advances theoretical understanding
% Practical applications of your research
    The results presented have several implications. Theoretically, they demonstrate that SAEs can offer more precise steering and control than might be possible with the base model alone, by disentangling general goal signals into more specific, identity-aware features. The influence of these features can be conditional on the agent's policy, providing a targeted control mechanism.

    Furthermore, this work illustrates how SAEs may compress specific functionalities into individual channels, and then encode further nuanced information (like entity identity) into the specific activation strength of those channels. This points towards more complex information processing strategies within neural networks than often assumed and has implications for understanding representational efficiency and the nature of learned features.

    Practically, these findings may provide insight into the application of SAEs more broadly beyond language models, and help to shed light on the internal operations of reinforcement learning models. Understanding these mechanisms is a step towards building more reliable, predictable, and ultimately safer AI systems.

\section{Recommendations}
\label{sec:recommendations}
% Suggestions for researchers and practitioners
% Policy implications if relevant
    Based on the findings, practitioners applying SAEs to RL agents should be aware of the potential for phenomena like dead channel steerability and activation-level encoding, as these may hold important clues to the model's function. Researchers are encouraged to further investigate the generalizability of these findings across different model architectures, RL environments, and SAE training configurations.
    A key recommendation stemming from this work is to not solely rely on coarse measures of channel activity (e.g., simple activation vs. no activation) but to consider the possibility of information being encoded in the precise magnitude of activations, especially when using tools like SAEs that promote sparse and disentangled representations.

\section{Final Remarks}
\label{sec:final_remarks}
% Closing thoughts on the research
% Broader impact of the work
    This research contributes to the growing field of mechanistic interpretability in reinforcement learning by providing a detailed analysis of goal representation and control in a complex environment. The application of Sparse Autoencoders has revealed nuanced ways in which information can be encoded and manipulated within an RL agent's neural network. The phenomena observed, such as entity multiplexing through activation strength and the functional relevance of dead channels, open up new avenues for understanding and potentially controlling sophisticated AI systems. Ultimately, continued exploration of these internal mechanisms is crucial for advancing our ability to develop AI that is not only capable but also transparent and trustworthy.
