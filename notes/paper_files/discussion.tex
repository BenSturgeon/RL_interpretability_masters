\chapter{Discussion}
\label{ch:discussion}

\section{Interpretation of Results}
\label{sec:interpretation}

\subsection{Activation Patterns and Spatial Gating}
Our findings in Section~\ref{sec:parallel_rollouts}, \ref{sec:offset_steering}, and \ref{sec:negative_activations} reveal that the network tracks game state through systematic changes in activation patterns. Rather than requiring separate channels for each entity type, the same channels are active throughout, but at different activation levels depending on game progress.

The ability to completely redirect agent behaviour through uniform activation offsets (93\% blue key collection shifting to 94\% green key collection in conv4a) demonstrates this is fundamental to the network's learned algorithm. However, the mechanism appears to be spatial gating rather than direct entity encoding: negative activations mark regions of interest, and early game stages have more negative activations overall. Shifting the offset thus mimics being at a different stage of the game, rather than directly specifying which entity to target. This suggests that interventions on neural networks need to consider not just which channels to modify, but how activation levels interact with spatial gating mechanisms.

This contrasts with \citep{mini_2023_understanding} (discussed in Section~\ref{sec:Understanding}), who found dedicated `cheese channels' that could be individually ablated. In our multi-objective setting, the network instead developed a spatial gating strategy, reusing the same channels across entities rather than dedicating separate channels to each. This suggests that representational strategies may differ substantially between single-goal and multi-goal environments.

Standard activation steering approaches~\citep{turner_2024_activation,mini_2023_understanding} require computing steering vectors from contrastive activation pairs. Our results suggest a simpler mechanism: negative activations mark regions of interest, directing the agent's attention without requiring entity-specific steering vectors. This may reflect a general-purpose strategy where networks use activation polarity to gate spatial attention, clearing irrelevant regions by shifting them toward zero while maintaining focus on task-relevant areas through negative activation.

Given the task similarity, repurposing entity-detection channels is efficient, allowing computational reuse. This strategy likely emerges from efficiency pressures during training wherein the core navigation task is exactly the same regardless of which entity is being targeted.

One challenge in making this discovery was that many of the tools typically used in mechanistic interpretability were not designed to expose temporal patterns in activation levels. SAEs and probes successfully identified which channels responded to which entities, but did not reveal how activation levels shifted systematically over the course of a rollout. What initially appeared to be polysemanticity was instead a structured spatial gating strategy. This differs from the superposition described by \citep{elhage2022superposition}, where features are compressed into overlapping directions. In our case, the same channels serve multiple game states through systematic variation in activation levels that gates spatial attention.



\subsection{Two-Phase Information Processing Architecture}
The probe results reveal why CNN-FC hybrid architectures are well suited for goal-directed navigation. CNNs capture and classify spatial relationships necessary for identifying `what is where', while fully-connected layers performed the task of integrating this information into coherent actions. Following \citep{alain_2018_understanding}, we use linear probes to assess where information crystallises in the network. Our finding that entity information peaks at conv3a (99.3\%) before collapsing in FC layers aligns with their observation that intermediate layers often contain the most linearly separable representations.

Prior work on RL interpretability has focused on saliency methods~\citep{greydanus_2018_visualizing} and feature visualisation~\citep{hilton_2020_understanding}. Our contribution extends this by revealing that activation polarity serves as a spatial gating mechanism, with negative activations marking regions of interest and this pattern shifting systematically as objectives are completed. While saliency methods identify where the network attends, they do not capture this temporal structure in how attention is released over the course of a rollout.

The inverse relationship between entity and directional information across layers suggests the network may perform active transformation rather than passive feature extraction. Conv4a's role as a bridge between the spatial and fully-connected layers is illustrated by the non-monotonic increase in the linear separability of the direction in which to go from conv3a to fc2. This implies that the linear separability drops due to an active transformation taking place in the structure of the information in conv4a. Future work in understanding this transformation would be valuable in establishing how RL agents transform visual information into motor output.

This crystallisation of goal commitment occurs at conv3a/conv4a. Our patching experiments target the precise point where the network commits to its goal, before that commitment disperses into the distributed representations necessary for action selection. Interventions earlier would affect raw perception; interventions later would fight against already-distributed action plans.

\subsection{SAE Validation and Dead Channels}
The majority of steerable SAE channels were `dead channels': channels that had zero activations during rollouts. Based on standard understanding of SAE training dynamics, we attribute this to the following mechanism: these encoder-decoder pairs initially encoded useful signal, but were subsequently suppressed as the L1 sparsity penalty forced lower activations. Crucially, while the encoder weights were driven toward near-zero values, the corresponding decoder weights would not change substantially during this process, retaining the directional structure learned when the latent was active. When we artificially activate these dead latents during intervention experiments, the preserved decoder weights still project meaningful directions into the activation space, enabling redirection of the agent despite these channels never activating during normal operation. More importantly, SAEs preserved the same activation patterns found in the base model rather than decomposing them into cleaner features, suggesting this spatial gating strategy is fundamental rather than an artifact of polysemanticity.

Unlike \citep{gorton_2024_missing}, who found SAEs successfully disentangled polysemantic features in InceptionV1, our SAEs preserved the same activation patterns rather than decomposing them into cleaner per-entity features. This may reflect fundamental differences between supervised classification, where features are stable across inputs, and RL, where the same visual features serve different behavioural roles depending on task state.

\subsection{Solution Multiplicity and Representational Drift}
Intervention positions changed completely across checkpoints even after convergence. Despite stable performance, the network continuously explored different encoding schemes, likely driven by training on infinite maze variations. The spatial gating pattern appears consistently, but which specific channels implement it drifts across checkpoints.

This suggests the network exists on a `valley floor' of equally viable solutions, constantly reorganizing internal representations while preserving behavioural performance. One checkpoint (step 30001) showed no steerable channels for any entity, the only such case in 60 checkpoints tested, suggesting this configuration may be less optimal than those with stronger intervention structure.

Training required unlimited procedural generation; attempts with 200 or 500 fixed mazes failed to train successfully, indicating the diversity was necessary for this architecture. 


\subsection{Conditions Where Activation Multiplexing Is Effective}

We hypothesise that this finding has particular relevance to RL agents trained in environments that rely on specific state variables in order to operate. For such problems, we identify two features of the environment that may encourage activation multiplexing. First, the same mechanism is used repeatedly in each stage, meaning sharing resources wherever possible is optimal. Second, there is strong pressure to correctly distinguish between entities. For example, in the case of Heist it will always use the same circuitry to navigate to the next entity, regardless of its nature, and if it targets the wrong entity it will almost certainly fail to solve the maze, meaning the costs for incorrect targeting is very high. This means taking radical measures to delineate the target by shifting the activation space entirely of many of the channels is an effective strategy.

\subsection{How The Network Selects Targets}

Modifying a single channel in a single layer is sufficient to completely alter behaviour, revealing extreme redundancy in the steering mechanism. The network uses negative activations to mark regions of interest, while near-zero or positive activations indicate regions to avoid. Mean activations remain well below zero for most channels during standard rollouts (Figure~\ref{fig:activation_rollout_all_channels}), consistent with this spatial gating interpretation.

Clamping experiments confirm this mechanism: setting activations to zero in specific regions completely prevents the agent from entering them, regardless of which entity occupies that region. This explains why artificially activating almost any region can redirect the agent; it introduces a signal the navigation system interprets as a region of interest. As entities are collected, fewer regions require suppression, explaining the systematic increase in mean activation values over the course of a rollout. Offset steering likely works by shifting the network into activation patterns characteristic of different game stages, changing which regions are marked as targets.

Past conv1a, the network does not rely on specialised channels for target selection. Channels in conv2a, conv3a, and conv4a play critical roles for multiple entities, with target selection determined by overall activation range rather than channel identity. Some specialisation exists in conv4a (channels 24, 25, 26 focus on red key and gem in Figure~\ref{fig:steering-dot-plot}), but this is the exception rather than the rule. 


\section{Theoretical Implications}
\label{sec:theoretical}

Spatial gating through activation polarity suggests neural networks employ more nuanced encoding strategies than simple feature presence/absence. Rather than dedicating separate channels to each goal, this network uses the analog nature of activations to gate attention across spatial regions, with negative values marking regions of interest. This challenges simpler models of feature representation and highlights activation-based gating as a potential principle of neural computation under representational efficiency pressure.

Standard MI tools (probes, SAEs) successfully identified which channels responded to which entities, but did not reveal how activation levels shifted systematically over rollouts. The spatial gating mechanism was discovered through mean activation analysis, not sophisticated interpretability tools. This suggests that MI approaches focused on feature identification may miss organisational principles encoded in temporal activation patterns.

For RL interpretability specifically, techniques successful in LLMs or simpler environments may need adaptation in complex, interactive settings. The interplay between learned environmental structure (sequential objectives) and emergent network representations is a key area for future theoretical development.

\section{Practical Applications}\label{sec:applications}

Small activation offsets ($\pm$0.5 at conv2a) can completely redirect agent behaviour while preserving navigation, suggesting a lightweight control mechanism requiring no architectural changes or retraining. Whether this generalises to other models remains to be tested, but detecting such patterns is straightforward (tracking mean activations over rollouts), making investigation low-cost. Training procedures could potentially be designed to encourage such structures, yielding models that are controllable by design.

The two-phase processing architecture provides a practical debugging framework: target convolutional layers for goal selection issues, FC layers for navigation and planning problems. Representations are most malleable early in the network where they first integrate low-level information, providing an optimal control point before entity selection disperses into distributed action plans.

\section{Methodological Insights}\label{sec:reflections}

A key methodological observation from this work is that the spatial gating mechanism was discovered through simple mean activation analysis rather than sophisticated interpretability tools. While significant effort was invested in training and analysing SAEs, the primary insights stemmed from tracking how activation levels changed over the course of rollouts. Initially, apparent polysemanticity in the channels motivated the use of SAEs to disentangle features. However, the network was not truly polysemantic in the conventional sense; rather, it recycled circuits by creating unique conditions through systematic shifts in activation ranges. What appeared to be polysemanticity was instead a structured encoding strategy.

This suggests that for future interpretability research, simple statistical approaches examining activation patterns over time may reveal organisational principles that more complex tools miss. In particular, the T-maze experimental design (Figure~\ref{fig:parallel-rollout}) proved valuable by allowing isolation of key variables, and analysing activations over complete rollouts provided far more insight than examining individual observations in isolation.
