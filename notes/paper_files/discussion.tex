\section{Interpretation of Results}
\label{sec:interpretation}

\subsection{Activation Patterns and Spatial Gating}
Our findings in Section~\ref{sec:parallel_rollouts}, \ref{sec:offset_steering}, and \ref{sec:negative_activations} reveal that the network tracks game state through systematic changes in activation patterns. Rather than requiring separate channels for each entity type, the same channels are active throughout, but at different activation levels depending on game progress.

The ability to completely redirect agent behaviour through uniform activation offsets (93\% blue key collection shifting to 94\% green key collection in conv4a) demonstrates this is fundamental to the network's learned algorithm. However, the mechanism appears to be spatial gating rather than direct entity encoding: negative activations mark regions of interest, and early game stages have more negative activations overall. Shifting the offset thus mimics being at a different stage of the game, rather than directly specifying which entity to target. This suggests that interventions on neural networks need to consider not just which channels to modify, but how activation levels interact with spatial gating mechanisms.

This contrasts with \citep{mini_2023_understanding}, who found dedicated `cheese channels' that could be individually ablated. In our multi-objective setting, the network instead developed a spatial gating strategy, reusing the same channels across entities rather than dedicating separate channels to each. This suggests that representational strategies may differ substantially between single-goal and multi-goal environments.

Standard activation steering approaches~\citep{turner_2024_activation,mini_2023_understanding} require computing steering vectors from contrastive activation pairs. Our results suggest a simpler mechanism: negative activations mark regions of interest, directing the agent's attention without requiring entity-specific steering vectors. This may reflect a general-purpose strategy where networks use activation polarity to gate spatial attention, clearing irrelevant regions by shifting them toward zero while maintaining focus on task-relevant areas through negative activation.

Due to the similarity of the task of tracking each key, it proves efficient to repurpose channels already capable of entity detection, allowing the reuse of computational machinery across different game stages. This strategy likely emerges from efficiency pressures during training wherein the core navigation task is exactly the same regardless of which entity is being targeted.

One challenge in making this discovery was that many of the tools typically used in mechanistic interpretability were not designed to expose temporal patterns in activation levels. SAEs and probes successfully identified which channels responded to which entities, but did not reveal how activation levels shifted systematically over the course of a rollout. What initially appeared to be polysemanticity was instead a structured spatial gating strategy. This differs from the superposition described by \citep{elhage2022superposition}, where features are compressed into overlapping directions. In our case, the same channels serve multiple game states through systematic variation in activation levels that gates spatial attention.



\subsection{Two-Phase Information Processing Architecture}
The probe results reveal why CNN-FC hybrid architectures are well suited for goal-directed navigation. CNNs capture and classify spatial relationships necessary for identifying `what is where', while fully-connected layers performed the task of integrating this information into coherent actions. Following \citep{alain_2018_understanding}, we use linear probes to assess where information crystallises in the network. Our finding that entity information peaks at conv3a (99.3\%) before collapsing in FC layers aligns with their observation that intermediate layers often contain the most linearly separable representations.

Prior work on RL interpretability has focused on saliency methods~\citep{greydanus_2018_visualizing} and feature visualisation~\citep{hilton_2020_understanding}. Our contribution extends this by revealing that activation polarity serves as a spatial gating mechanism, with negative activations marking regions of interest and this pattern shifting systematically as objectives are completed. While saliency methods identify where the network attends, they do not capture this temporal structure in how attention is released over the course of a rollout.

The inverse relationship between entity and directional information across layers suggests the network may perform active transformation rather than passive feature extraction. Conv4a's role as a bridge between the spatial and fully-connected layers is illustrated by the non-monotonic increase in the linear separability of the direction in which to go from conv3a to fc2. This implies that the linear separability drops due to an active transformation taking place in the structure of the information in conv4a. Future work in understanding this transformation would be valuable in establishing how RL agents transform visual information into motor output.

This crystallisation of goal commitment occurs at conv3a/conv4a. Our patching experiments target the precise point where the network commits to its goal, before that commitment disperses into the distributed representations necessary for action selection. Interventions earlier would affect raw perception; interventions later would fight against already-distributed action plans.

\subsection{SAE Validation and Dead Channels}
The majority of steerable SAE channels were `dead channels', channels that had zero activations during rollouts. Based on standard understanding of SAE training dynamics, we attribute this to the following mechanism: these encoder-decoder pairs initially encoded useful signal, but were subsequently suppressed as the L1 sparsity penalty forced lower activations. Crucially, while the encoder weights were driven toward near-zero values, the corresponding decoder weights would not change substantially during this process, retaining the directional structure learned when the latent was active. When we artificially activate these dead latents during intervention experiments, the preserved decoder weights still project meaningful directions into the activation space, enabling redirection of the agent despite these channels never activating during normal operation. More importantly, SAEs preserved the same activation patterns found in the base model rather than decomposing them into cleaner features, suggesting this spatial gating strategy is fundamental rather than an artifact of polysemanticity.

Unlike \citep{gorton_2024_missing}, who found SAEs successfully disentangled polysemantic features in InceptionV1, our SAEs preserved the same activation patterns rather than decomposing them into cleaner per-entity features. This may reflect fundamental differences between supervised classification, where features are stable across inputs, and RL, where the same visual features serve different behavioural roles depending on task state.

\subsection{Solution Multiplicity and Representational Drift}
A striking observation from our experiments was that intervention positions changed completely across different checkpoints even after convergence. Despite maintaining similar performance, the network continued exploring different encoding schemes within the solution space. This is largely due to the fact that we trained the model with an endless variety of maze variations rather than a fixed number, meaning it continued to adapt and change over time. This configuration was also the only one that led to successful training of our smaller model.

This variety of solutions suggests that while the spatial gating pattern appears consistently, the specific channels used are not fixed but drift across checkpoints. The continuous drift between encoding schemes post-convergence indicates the network exists on a `valley floor' of equally viable solutions, constantly reorganizing its internal representations while preserving behavioural performance. 

We find a notable checkpoint at update step 30001 where no steerable channels at all are found for any entity, though we only found 1 example of this in the 60 checkpoints we tested, suggesting it might be less optimal than those with the stronger intervention configuration. 


\subsection{Conditions Where Activation Multiplexing Is Effective}

We hypothesise that this finding has particular relevance to RL agents trained in environments that rely on specific state variables in order to operate. For such problems, we identify two features of the environment that may encourage activation multiplexing. First, the same mechanism is used repeatedly in each stage, meaning sharing resources wherever possible is optimal. Second, there is strong pressure to correctly distinguish between entities. For example, in the case of Heist it will always use the same circuitry to navigate to the next entity, regardless of its nature, and if it targets the wrong entity it will almost certainly fail to solve the maze, meaning the costs for incorrect targeting is very high. This means taking radical measures to delineate the target by shifting the activation space entirely of many of the channels is an effective strategy.

While highly speculative, a similar phenomenon could occur in other networks when distinct modes of operation appear. For example, in the case of reasoning LLMs, there may be benefit to multiplexing activations in certain channels to indicate whether the model is currently in the reasoning stage or in the actual output step. By doing this, certain signals could uniquely activate words such that they have different meanings in the output compared to when they are used in the chain of thought. Further work in this area could be highly instructive, though substantial differences between convolutional and transformer architectures may limit direct transferability.

\subsection{How The Network Selects Targets}

We were surprised by the extreme degree of redundancy of steering that could be found in this network, whereby modifying a single channel in a single layer was sufficient to completely alter behaviour. Our analysis in Section~\ref{sec:negative_activations} reveals that spatial navigation relies on activation polarity: the network uses negative activations to mark regions of interest, while near-zero or positive activations indicate regions to avoid. Evidence of this is provided in the mean activations generally being well below 0 for most channels during a standard rollout, as seen in Figure~\ref{fig:activation_rollout_all_channels}.

The clamping experiments confirm this spatial gating mechanism: setting activations to zero in specific regions completely prevents the agent from entering them. The spatial gating explains why artificially activating almost any region can redirect the agent: it introduces a signal that the navigation system interprets as a region of interest. As entities are collected and removed from the environment, fewer regions require suppression, explaining the systematic increase in mean activation values observed over the course of a rollout. The offset steering effect likely works by shifting the network into activation patterns characteristic of different game stages, which changes which regions are marked as regions of interest.

It also seems to be the case that past conv1a, the network generally does not rely heavily on specialised channels for selecting the next target, but rather that many of the channels in conv2a, conv3a, and conv4a play critical roles for multiple or all entities, and that which entity they are currently attending to is largely determined by the overall activation range in the inputs from previous layers. We do see small amounts of specialisation in conv4a with certain channels being quite focused on one or two entities, such as channels 24, 25, and 26 in Figure~\ref{fig:steering-dot-plot} primarily being responsible for the red key and gem. 


\section{Theoretical Implications}
\label{sec:theoretical}
% How your results contribute to theory
% New insights about RL interpretability
The findings from this research contribute to the theoretical understanding of mechanistic interpretability in deep reinforcement learning in several ways. The demonstration of spatial gating through activation polarity suggests that neural networks may employ more nuanced information encoding strategies than simple feature presence/absence, utilizing the analog nature of activations to gate attention across spatial regions. This challenges some simpler models of feature representation and highlights the potential for activation-based gating as a core principle of neural computation, especially when under pressure for representational efficiency.

The dead channel phenomenon in SAEs, and its mechanistic explanation, provides a concrete example of how network components that appear non-functional during standard inference can retain latent capabilities and learned knowledge. This has implications for how we assess model capacity and robustness, and for understanding the learning dynamics that lead to such states.

One observation from carrying out this work is that uncovering the spatial gating mechanism was difficult using typical mechanistic interpretability tools such as probes and SAEs. The most important discovery regarding how the network functioned (the role of negative activations in marking regions of interest) turned out to be explicable purely by analysing the mean activations of different channels over the course of a rollout. It is possible that there may be further interesting discoveries to be made using similar approaches, particularly in networks that would likely benefit from shared functionality for similar tasks that require clear delineation.

For RL interpretability specifically, this work underscores that techniques successful in one domain (e.g., LLMs) or simpler environments may need adaptation or re-evaluation in more complex, interactive settings. The interplay between learned environmental structure (like sequential objectives in Heist) and emergent network representations is a key area for future theoretical development.

\section{Practical Applications}\label{sec:applications}
The discovery that this network uses spatial gating through activation polarity may have implications if the effect is observed to generalise to other models. Given the level of steering that was possible through small offset adjustments (±0.5 at conv2a), the technique has the potential to offer a lightweight control mechanism that does not require architectural changes or retraining. While it remains to be seen to what extent other neural networks have similar gating mechanisms, the potential upside of the flexibility and ease of determining whether it is present means that investigating models for this pattern could have immediate practical benefits. It could even be worth structuring environments and training in such a way as to encourage the development of such a structure in order to allow for greater control via precise intervention, particularly if the mechanism is better understood.

As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:interpretation}, the two-phase processing architecture suggests that interventions should target specific layers depending on the desired effect: earlier convolutional layers for goal selection, FC layers for navigation and planning. This layer-specific approach could make it simpler to debug models by focusing attention on the relevant portion of the network. However, there are still significant challenges in understanding the mechanisms at play in the FC layers of this network.

Most importantly, understanding that representations are most malleable early in the network where they first integrate low level information provides an optimal control point, rather than trying shift the course of the model after entity selection has already occurred.

\section{Reflections on the Research Process}\label{sec:reflections}

This research undertook many iterations, with the most important insight only occurring very close to the end, discovering the importance of the activation values as being key for entity targeting. Early hints of this were present in the form of visualising the activations and seeing the shift in activations as different entities were collected, but rigorously showing this with a simple experiment was a significant breakthrough that led to real insight. The key insight that led to this was from the steering activation ranges, and their multiplexed structure. Testing this result rigorously for validity led to the observation of the stronger activation offset phenomenon.

One of the most important reflections of this was how relying on existing MI techniques failed to provide a good way of discovering this. A great deal of our effort and research time went towards the SAEs and implementing them, but the majority of real insights came from simple techniques such as probes, and our mean activation offset analysis. 

If our contribution of simply observing mean activations across different parts of the network can be transferred to other models and contexts, this would be very encouraging and may lead to new ways of thinking about models. The simplicity of the technique means that it theoretically can be applied to almost any model, though it will likely be highly variable in its usefulness. The abstraction of channels in our convolutional layers were useful high levels units to examine, and which are not present in many model architectures. 

Another insight was that the idea of the network being too polysemantic to be a good target for analysis also led to discouragement on the viability of the project as a whole, and led to the implementation of the SAEs. The later insight was the network was not truly polysemantic, but that it recycled circuits by creating unique conditions through the activation ranges being used at any given time. The `polysemanticity' was an important hint of this mechanism at play.

For people who aim to do similar work in future, a critical learning we would want to share would be the need for simple experiments, such as the structure of the T-maze in~\ref{fig:parallel-rollout} which allowed us to isolate the key variables. It was also essential to focus on the structure of activations over the entire course of a rollout, which provided far more information than what could be gleaned from analysing individual observations and their respective activations. The context provided by the full rollout was essential to understanding the network.
