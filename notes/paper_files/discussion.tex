\chapter{Discussion}
\label{ch:discussion}

\section{Interpretation of Results}
\label{sec:interpretation}

The literature review identified four gaps in RL interpretability research (Section~\ref{sec:gaps}): how agents represent and switch between multiple sequential objectives, whether SAEs disentangle representations in RL CNNs as effectively as in supervised vision models, and whether activation patterns beyond simple feature presence carry goal-relevant information. Our findings address these gaps through the discovery of a spatial gating mechanism where negative activations mark regions of current interest, with systematic shifts tracking task progress.

\subsection{Activation Patterns and Spatial Gating}
Our findings in Section~\ref{sec:parallel_rollouts}, \ref{sec:offset_steering}, and \ref{sec:negative_activations} reveal that the network tracks game states through systematic changes in activation patterns. Rather than requiring separate channels for each entity type, the same channels are active throughout, but at different activation levels depending on game progress.

The ability to completely redirect agent behaviour through uniform activation offsets (93\% blue key collection shifting to 94\% green key collection in conv4a) suggests that this is central to the network's learned algorithm. This suggests that interventions on neural networks need to consider not just which channels to modify, but how activation levels interact with correlations in state the model has learned. The effectiveness in taking advantage of this correlation in directly affecting behaviour is one of the most surprising results in our research, and presents some concerns on the effects of confounders in causal methods in MI.

This contrasts with the single objective setting of \citet{mini_2023_understanding} (discussed in Section~\ref{sec:Understanding}), who found dedicated `cheese channels' that could be individually ablated to retarget the network. In the multi-objective setting, the network reuses the same channels across entities rather than dedicating separate channels to each, with activation levels encoding which entity is currently relevant. This suggests that representational strategies may differ substantially between single-goal and multi-goal environments.

One challenge in making this discovery was that many of the tools typically used in mechanistic interpretability were not designed to expose temporal patterns in activation levels. SAEs and probes successfully identified which channels responded to which entities, but did not reveal how activation levels shifted systematically over the course of a rollout. This differs from the superposition described by \citet{elhage2022superposition} (Section~\ref{subsec:superposition}), where features are compressed into overlapping directions because they rarely co-occur in training data. In our case, all entities are simultaneously visible in the maze; the network is not compressing features that don't co-occur, but tracking which of several co-occurring features is currently behaviourally relevant through variation in activation strength.



\subsection{Two-Phase Information Processing Architecture}
The probe results reveal why CNN-FC hybrid architectures are well suited for goal-directed navigation. CNNs capture and classify spatial relationships necessary for identifying `what is where', while fully-connected layers performed the task of integrating this information into coherent actions, which in itself is not surprising. Following \citet{alain_2018_understanding}, we use linear probes to assess where information crystallises in the network. Our finding that entity information peaks at conv3a (99.3\%) before collapsing in FC layers aligns with their observation that intermediate layers often contain the most linearly separable representations.

The inverse relationship between entity and directional information across layers suggests the network may perform active transformation rather than passive feature extraction. Conv4a's role as a bridge between the spatial and fully-connected layers is illustrated by the non-monotonic increase in the linear separability of the direction in which to go from conv3a to fc2. This implies that the linear separability drops due to an active transformation taking place in the structure of the information in conv4a. Future work in understanding this transformation would be valuable in establishing how RL agents transform visual information into motor output.

This crystallisation of goal commitment occurs at conv3a/conv4a. Our patching experiments target the precise point where the network commits to its goal, before that commitment disperses into the distributed representations necessary for action selection. Interventions earlier would affect raw perception; interventions later would fight against already-distributed action plans.

\subsection{Solution Multiplicity and Representational Drift}
Intervention positions changed completely across checkpoints even after convergence. Despite stable performance, the network continuously explored different encoding schemes, likely driven by training on infinite maze variations. The spatial gating pattern appears consistently, but which specific channels implement it drifts across checkpoints.

This drift suggests that the network exists on a `valley floor' of equally viable solutions, constantly reorganizing internal representations while preserving behavioural performance. One checkpoint (step 30001) showed no steerable channels for any entity, the only such case in 60 checkpoints tested, suggesting that this configuration may be less optimal than those with stronger intervention structure.

Training required unlimited procedural generation; attempts with 200 or 500 fixed mazes failed to train successfully, indicating the diversity was necessary for this architecture. 


\subsection{Environmental Conditions That May Encourage This Strategy}

Two features of Heist may have encouraged the encoding strategies we observe. First, the same navigation mechanism is used repeatedly across stages, making resource sharing optimal. Second, there is strong pressure to correctly distinguish between entities: targeting the wrong entity almost certainly causes failure. These pressures may explain why the network developed systematic activation-level shifts to delineate targets, though we cannot confirm this was the only viable solution.

\subsection{How The Network Selects Targets}

Modifying a single channel in a single layer is sufficient to alter behaviour, revealing redundancy in the steering mechanism. Mean activations remain well below zero for most channels during standard rollouts (Figure~\ref{fig:activation_rollout_all_channels}).

Clamping experiments confirm this mechanism: setting activations to zero in specific regions completely prevents the agent from entering them, regardless of which entity occupies that region. While positive activation patching can also redirect the agent, the mechanism by which this works is less clear, since positive activations should indicate regions to avoid under the spatial gating hypothesis. As entities are collected, fewer regions require suppression, explaining the systematic increase in mean activation values over the course of a rollout.

The activation-magnitude patterns we observe in steering experiments appear to be a downstream correlation rather than the causal selection mechanism itself. Because spatial gating produces predictable magnitude shifts as each stage clears (fewer negative regions = higher mean activation), the network has learned deep statistical correlations between overall activation magnitude and game progress. Offset steering exploits these correlations rather than directly manipulating the selection mechanism. This distinction is important: successful steering does not imply that we have identified the causal mechanism, only that we have found an exploitable correlation.

Past conv1a, the network does not rely on specialised channels for target selection. Channels in conv2a, conv3a, and conv4a play critical roles for multiple entities, with target selection determined by overall activation range rather than channel identity. Some specialisation exists in conv4a (channels 24, 25, 26 focus on red key and gem in Figure~\ref{fig:steering-dot-plot}), but this is the exception rather than the rule.

\subsection{Ablation Studies}

The ablation results (Section~\ref{sec:ablation_results}) show navigation is redundantly encoded: many channels retain some ability to reach entities when all others are masked (Figure~\ref{fig:ablation-heatmap}). Results were often counterintuitive, with targeted ablations sometimes improving performance on non-targeted entities. This complexity likely reflects the shared circuitry serving multiple goals in this environment.

\section{Theoretical Implications}
\label{sec:theoretical}

The spatial gating mechanism we observe suggests that within multi-objective settings, the network employs more nuanced encoding strategies than simple feature presence/absence. Rather than dedicating separate channels to each goal, it uses negative activations to mark spatial regions of interest, with the same channels active across different game states. Whether similar strategies emerge in other architectures remains an open question.

As noted above, this mechanism was discovered through mean activation analysis rather than sophisticated interpretability tools, suggesting that MI approaches focused on feature identification may miss organisational principles encoded in activation dynamics.

For RL interpretability specifically, techniques successful in LLMs or simpler environments may need adaptation in complex, interactive settings. The interplay between learned environmental structure (sequential objectives) and emergent network representations is a key area for future theoretical development.

\section{Practical Applications}\label{sec:applications}

The precision of offset steering ($\pm$0.5 at conv2a is sufficient) suggests a lightweight control mechanism requiring no architectural changes or retraining. However, this technique exploits correlations between activation levels and game states that emerged from Heist's particular sequential structure. Other models would need to develop analogous correlations for similar interventions to work, and there is no guarantee that they would. Detecting such patterns is straightforward (tracking mean activations over rollouts), but finding exploitable correlations in other settings should not be assumed.

The two-phase processing architecture provides a practical debugging framework: target convolutional layers for goal selection issues, FC layers for navigation and planning problems. Representations are most malleable early in the network where they first integrate low-level information, providing an optimal control point before entity selection disperses into distributed action plans.

\section{Methodological Insights}\label{sec:reflections}

While significant effort was invested in training and analysing SAEs, the primary insights stemmed from tracking how activation levels changed over the course of rollouts. Initially, apparent polysemanticity in the channels motivated the use of SAEs to disentangle features. However, the network was not truly polysemantic in the conventional sense; rather, it recycled circuits by creating unique conditions through systematic shifts in activation ranges. What appeared to be polysemanticity was instead a structured encoding strategy.

For future interpretability research, the T-maze experimental design (Figure~\ref{fig:parallel-rollout}) proved valuable by allowing isolation of key variables, and analysing activations over complete rollouts provided far more insight than examining individual observations in isolation.
