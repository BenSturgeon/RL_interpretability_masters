\section{Introduction}\label{sec:lit_intro}
The previous chapter established the motivation for this research, highlighting the need to better understand the internal workings of Reinforcement Learning (RL) agents, particularly in multi-objective environments. This chapter provides a review of the foundational literature that underpins this thesis.

This review will cover the fundamentals of Reinforcement Learning relevant concepts in mechanistic interpretability (MI). It will then delve into the specific challenges and recent advancements in making RL agents more understandable. In particular we examine the foundational works upon which this research rests from the fundamental RL concepts to the algorithms used to train the models. The rest of this section will situate the present research within the research landscape and seek to demonstrate how it builds upon prior work in the field of MI to address unsolved problems in RL interpretability.

\section{Reinforcement Learning Fundamentals}\label{sec:rl_fundamentals}
% Overview of basic RL concepts
% Key algorithms and approaches in RL
This section provides a brief overview of the fundamental concepts in RL that are relevant to the research discussed. While a baseline understanding of RL and AI concepts is assumed, key elements of the environments and training paradigms employed are highlighted.

\subsection{Markov Decision Processes}\label{subsec:mdp}
% Mathematical framework for RL
% Definitions and properties
At its core, the problem that RL solves in general can be formalized as a Markov Decision Process (MDP). An MDP is a mathematical framework for modeling decision-making in situations where outcomes are partially random and partially determined by the policy of the agent. It is defined by a tuple (\(S, A, P, R, \gamma\)), representing the set of states, the set of actions, the state transition probability function \(P(s'|s,a)\), the reward function \(R(s,a,s')\), and a discount factor \(\gamma \in [0, 1]\) that balances immediate and future rewards. The agent's goal is to learn a strategy that maximizes the cumulative discounted reward over time. Because the agent receives the entire 64 by 64 pixel view of the maze as its observation, the Procgen Heist environment is a fully-observable MDP, where the state is directly represented by the agent's visual input. There are variations of the environment which involved a limited view of the environment such as the `memory' distribution, which would be considered a partially observable MDP (POMDP).

\subsection{Value Functions and Policies}\label{subsec:value_policy}

The process of training a model involves iterating through many interactions with the environment to produce a policy denoted as \(\pi(a|s)\), which is a mapping from states to a probability distribution over actions. The goal of the RL training process is to find an optimal policy, \(\pi^*\), that maximises the agent's expected reward over time. As a means of estimating the quality of a given policy, RL functions often use value functions\cite{sutton2018reinforcement}. The state-value function, \(V^\pi(s)\), represents the expected return after taking an action \(a\) in state \(s\) based on following policy \(\pi\). The action-value function, \(Q^\pi(s,a)\), is the expected return from continuing to follow policy \(\pi\) after taking an action given a certain state. 

These functions are foundational to the function of training algorithms such as PPO which was used to train the agent in this research. PPO alternates between sampling data from the environment and using that data to optimise a `surrogate' objective function using stochastic gradient ascent.

\subsection{Deep Reinforcement Learning}\label{subsec:deep_rl}
% Integration of deep learning with RL
% Notable architectures and algorithms
Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) combines deep neural networks with RL principles to learn effective policies in complex, high-dimensional environments like Procgen. These models typically use convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to process visual input and feed the resulting features into fully-connected layers that output a policy.

The research this thesis is based on utilizes specific DRL architectures chosen for their balance of performance and interpretability. Following the precedent set by\cite{hilton_2020_understanding}, which demonstrated that simpler architectures often yield more interpretable representations without significant performance loss, the models used were simplified versions of established architectures. For example, the primary policy model used is a compressed version of the Impala architecture\citep{espeholt_2018_impala} with only 5 convolutional layers instead of the standard 15. This choice reflects the principle that simpler architectures are often easier to interpret\citep{hilton_2020_understanding}.

\section{Interpretability in Machine Learning}\label{sec:interpretability}
% Definition and importance of interpretability
% Different approaches to interpretability
Interpretability in machine learning refers to the extent to which a human can understand the cause and effect of a model's decisions. It is crucial for debugging, ensuring fairness, building trust, and verifying reliability, especially in high-stakes applications.

% Content from Paper 1: "Entity Multiplexing..."
Sparse AutoEncoders (SAEs) have proven to be a useful tool in understanding neural networks by acting as an unsupervised tool that can compress meaningful operations into more isolated circuitry. This allows for a reduction in the issue of neural network functionality being distributed across different regions within a layer\citep{elhage_toy_2022}, and facilitates a better understanding of the network's operations\citep{bricken2023monosemanticity}. Recent work has demonstrated how SAEs can reveal interpretable features in various neural architectures, including CNNs\citep{gorton_2024_missing} and transformers\citep{conerly_update_2024}.
% End Content from Paper 1


\subsection{Post-hoc Explanations}\label{subsec:post_hoc}
% Methods that explain already trained models
% Feature attribution, saliency maps, etc.
Post-hoc explanation methods aim to provide insights into already trained models without altering their architecture or training process. Examples include feature attribution methods (like LIME, SHAP), saliency maps, and activation steering.

% Content from Paper 2: "Discovering Goals..."
Activation steering, as demonstrated in \cite{mini_2023_understanding} and extended in the discussed research, is a post-hoc technique. Steering vectors, representing the difference in activations between states with and without an objective, can be used to influence a model's behavior.
% End Content from Paper 2

% Content from Paper 1: "Entity Multiplexing..."
The feature visualization methodologies discussed, such as synthetic visualization (optimizing an input image for maximal channel activation) and finding maximally activating dataset samples \citep{olah_feature_2017, hilton_understanding_2020}, are also forms of post-hoc analysis.
% End Content from Paper 1

\subsection{Intrinsically Interpretable Models}\label{subsec:intrinsic}
% Models designed to be interpretable from the ground up
Intrinsically interpretable models are designed from the outset to be understandable, often by constraining their complexity (e.g., linear models, decision trees) or by building in mechanisms that are inherently transparent. Training SAEs, as discussed in ``Entity Multiplexing Through Activation Strength'' can be seen as an effort to transform parts of a complex model into a more intrinsically interpretable representation by disentangling features.

\section{Interpretability in Reinforcement Learning}\label{sec:rl_interpretability}
% Current state of interpretability research specifically for RL
% Challenges unique to interpreting RL agents
Interpretability in RL presents unique challenges due to the agent's interaction with an environment, credit assignment over long time horizons, and often complex, high-dimensional state and action spaces.

% Content from Paper 1: "Entity Multiplexing..."
Previous work by\cite{miniUnderstandingControllingMazeSolving2023} demonstrated the ability to manipulate an agent's navigation in a simple maze environment with an intervention to a single channel in the network, serving as a foundation for extending these techniques to more complex multi-objective settings.
Work by\cite{betley_2023_localizing} found a specific circuit in the convolutional layers of the Impala model that activated strongly for specific directional goals, indicating that planning might occur in CNN layers.
% End Content from Paper 1

% Content from Paper 2: "Discovering Goals..."
In ``Understanding and Controlling a Maze-Solving Policy Network'' demonstrated that neural networks can develop interpretable mechanisms to track goals\cite{mini_2023_understanding}. The authors identified specific channels that tracked the location of objectives in a maze environment and showed that these could be manipulated to control the agent's behavior. They found that steering vectors, the difference in activations between otherwise identical states with and without an entity, could be used to influence the model's behavior in predictable ways. This work serves as the foundation for our research, though we extend it to a multi-objective setting. 

The larger circuits framework by\cite{olah_2020_zoom} provides methods to understand how neural networks combine simple features into complex representations. Their work on polysemantic neurons (neurons responding to multiple distinct features) is particularly relevant when an agent must represent multiple objectives\cite{elhage_toy_2022}.
% End Content from Paper 2

Work by\cite{betley_2023_localizing} found a specific circuit in the convolutional layers of the Impala model that activated strongly when the direction to go was `up' and didn't activate when the goal was in a similar spot but the agent would need to go in a different direction. This indicates that much of the `planning' was already handled in the CNN layers, suggesting it should be possible to train probes that can detect the exact direction the agent needs to move towards.

Work exploring the Procgen Coinrun environment provides a rich set of ideas for how to attribute attention from objects in the input to specific weights within the model, as well as visualizing the model's weights\cite{hilton_2020_understanding}. The most striking aspect of their work showing clear positive and negative attributions to specific entities according to how it might affect its ability to complete the task within the environment.

\section{The Procgen Suite of environments}\label{subsec:procgen}

The Procgen suite of environments consist of 16 different environments that rely on procedural generation to create diverse challenges for RL agents to learn to solve. They are inspired by Atari games and thus build upon existing games such as those used in the Arcade Learning Environment\cite{bellemare_2013_arcade}. This type of environment forces the agent to not overfit to a particular solution for playing the games effectively, and instead forces it to learn a robust, generalisable policy for each environment.

The Procgen Heist environment, the focus of the presented research, requires an agent to collect up to 3 keys and 3 locks before reaching the final goal, a gem\cite{cobbe_2020_leveraging}. The order in which the keys need to be collected is always the same (blue, green, red). The environment is procedurally generated and might generate with any combination of no keys, or 1--3 keys, as can be seen in\ref{fig:environment-overview}. The size of the maze will also change depending on how many objects are included. The environment provides sparse rewards, only giving out a score of 10 if the agent makes it all the way to the final goal and 0 otherwise. This establishes a natural curriculum for the agent and gives it a reasonable chance of reaching the end with a random walk before it starts to develop an actual policy.

This environment was selected due to the necessity for the agent to pursue multiple objectives within a single episode, likely influencing the development of generalizable maze-navigation circuits. It is a sparse learning environment.

\section{Gaps in the Literature}
\label{sec:gaps}
% Identify areas where current research is lacking
% Connect these gaps to your research objectives
Prior to the research detailed in the accompanying papers, several areas in RL interpretability remained open for exploration:

% Derived from Paper 1 Future Work & Discussion
\begin{itemize}
    \item Understanding how RL agents track and switch between multiple objectives, especially when those objectives are not the immediate next target.
    \item The application and efficacy of techniques like Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) for disentangling representations specifically within RL agent architectures, particularly CNNs.
    \item Investigating how fine-grained details, such as the precise activation strength within a neural channel, might encode complex information like entity identity or priority.
    \item The role and potential utility of "dead" channels in SAEs, which cease to activate during normal operation but might retain learned decoder weights.
    \item The generalization of interpretability findings from simpler RL agents to more complex architectures like decision transformers or RNN-based agents, especially for tasks requiring memory.
    \item The potential for more advanced attribution techniques, such as attribution-based parameter decomposition \citep{braun_2025_interpretability}, to yield insights in RL models.
    \item Clarifying whether functionalities preserved or highlighted by SAEs (e.g., entity encoding via activation strength) reflect similar underlying mechanisms in the base model.
\end{itemize}

% Derived from Paper 2 Discussion & Future Work
\begin{itemize}
    \item Many goal-related activations in RL agents can be entangled across channels, making it difficult to isolate the function of individual components without tools like SAEs or causal tracing.
    \item The behavior of interpretability interventions (like activation patching) in off-distribution settings for RL agents.
    \item Providing precise, exhaustive maps of how different channels contribute to goal representations and decision-making.
    \item The effect of environment design (e.g., fixed vs. variable ordering of objectives) on the development of generalizable circuits for goal pursuit.
    \item The challenge of interpreting precisely why certain activations occur at specific times, especially when functionality is distributed across a network.
    \item Systematically analyzing the role of individual channels (including "dead" channels in SAEs) in navigation and goal pursuit through methods like ablation studies and targeted interventions.
\end{itemize}

\section{Summary}\label{sec:lit_summary}
% Synthesize the key findings from the literature
% Explain how your research builds on existing work
The literature on interpretability in reinforcement learning highlights a growing interest in moving beyond performance metrics to understand the internal workings of RL agents. Foundational work has demonstrated the feasibility of identifying and manipulating neural correlates of an agent's goals and behaviors, particularly in visual navigation tasks \citep{mini_understanding_2023, hilton_understanding_2020}. Techniques such as activation analysis, steering vectors, and feature visualization have provided initial windows into these complex systems. However, challenges like polysemanticity (where neurons or channels respond to multiple unrelated concepts \citep{olah_2020_zoom}) and the distributed nature of representations often complicate direct interpretation.

The emergence of tools like Sparse Autoencoders offers a promising direction for disentangling these representations \citep{elhage_toy_2022, bricken_towards_2023}, potentially making the functional roles of different network components clearer. While much of the early MI work focused on LLMs, applying and adapting these techniques to the unique context of RL—with its emphasis on sequential decision-making, credit assignment, and interaction with dynamic environments—is an active area of research.

The research presented in the accompanying papers (forming the basis of this thesis) builds directly on this existing work by:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Extending activation steering and intervention techniques to more complex, multi-objective RL environments like Procgen Heist.
    \item Applying Sparse Autoencoders to the convolutional layers of an RL agent to disentangle features and investigate phenomena like polysemanticity and feature encoding through activation strength.
    \item Systematically analyzing the role of individual channels (including "dead" channels in SAEs) in navigation and goal pursuit through methods like ablation studies and targeted interventions.
    \item Exploring novel mechanisms of information encoding, such as multiplexing entity information within a single channel through varying activation strengths.
\end{itemize}
These contributions aim to deepen our understanding of how RL agents represent and pursue goals, and to refine the tools available for mechanistic interpretability in this domain.
