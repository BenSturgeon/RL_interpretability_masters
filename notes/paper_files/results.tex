\section{Parallel Rollout Activation Analysis}
\label{sec:parallel_rollouts}
Parallel rollouts reveal distinct activation trajectories for each entity type (Figure~\ref{fig:parallel-rollout}). The same channels are active across all entities, but at systematically different levels depending on which entity is being targeted. This distinguishes between two possible encoding strategies: entity-specific channels that uniquely fire for particular entities, versus shared channels where activation levels shift systematically with game state.

Clean parallel trajectories with significant gaps between entity types indicate strong value encoding for entity targeting. Channels with the lowest inter-entity offset show similar parallelism, though certain entities (e.g., red key and blue key) sometimes overlap, indicating less differentiation in those channels.

We observe extremely high correlation between activation patterns in both conv3a and conv4a. Conv3a shows an average correlation of 0.956 (range: 0.837-0.995). Conv4a has a comparable average (0.931) but contains a subset of channels with substantially lower correlations (range: 0.401-0.995). These low-correlation channels exhibit activation patterns that vary over time in ways that differ between entities, rather than maintaining a constant offset throughout the trajectory, suggesting they play an unusual role in entity targeting. \subsection{Activation patterns over rollouts}
We carried out further experiments to observe how activation patterns evolved over the course of a rollout as different entities were collected. We captured activations over the course of rollouts in both the custom maze environments and unmodified mazes, taking the mean activation in each channel at every timestep. This revealed clear patterns where activations would typically jump upwards as each key was collected, with the size of the jump decreasing after each collection. These jumps were particularly obvious in the highest-variance channels, with the effect present but not ubiquitous amongst other channels. Results of a randomly selected rollout are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:activation_tracking}.

Figure~\ref{fig:activation_tracking} illustrates a recurring `pinching' effect, where activations converge to similar values across entities. This is likely because the network has learned that regardless of the entity involved, if the player is one step away from the entity then it always makes sense to go directly to that entity, as in the normal course of a rollout there is no scenario in which it can be directly beside an entity while it would be incorrect to collect that entity, except in the case of locks. 

Interestingly, in the cross maze where it was possible to access all the keys from the beginning, the model would choose to target the green key first in about 12\% of cases (n=500 rollouts). In these instances we would still see the positive jump in activations as we saw in the case of the blue key, indicating that this phenomenon is primarily tied to the number of entities collected, rather than targeting a specific entity.

\section{Activation Offsets Achieve Steering}
\label{sec:offset_steering}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.98\textwidth]{figures/offset_sweep_conv4a_expanded_checkpoint_35001_full_range.pdf}
    \caption[Conv4a activation offset steering results]{Activation offset steering results for layer conv4a at checkpoint 35k. The x-axis shows the offset value applied uniformly across all channels, while the y-axis shows the percentage of rollouts where the agent first collected each entity type. Positive offsets induce green key targeting (peaking at 94\% at offset $+4.8$), while negative offsets shift behaviour towards red key collection (52\% at offset $-5.8$). Blue key collection dominates near baseline (93\% at offset 0.0). A run ends immediately upon contact with an entity, and is considered `none' if no entity is collected within 100 timesteps.}
    \label{fig:activation_offset_results}
\end{figure}


Surprisingly, a static offset to channel activations reliably steered the agent towards different entities than what the agent would target by default. Results of rates of initial pickup over different rollouts are presented in Figure~\ref{fig:activation_offset_results}. We also observe this result on different checkpoints, with different success rates for offset values but with similar patterns. 
We find that steering towards different entities by adjusting mean offset works across all layers, suggesting that this is endemic to the structure of the network and not simply an isolated phenomenon.

We observe complete shifts in entity targeting. Baseline rates show 93\% $\pm$ 5\% blue key targeting at offset 0.0. At positive offsets, the agent switches to targeting the green key, peaking at 94\% $\pm$ 5\% at offset $+4.8$. At negative offsets, we observe peak red key collection of 52\% $\pm$ 10\% at offset $-5.8$. Rates of not collecting any entity increase at the extremes, but overall collection remains fairly high, meaning important navigational functions continue to function.

Conv2a (shown in Figure~\ref{fig:activation_offset_results_conv2a_zoomed}) shows particularly amenable offset steering with fine grained control. Red key steering peaks at 75\% $\pm$ 5\% at offset $+0.30$, illustrating clear and strong steering. There is also a surprising surge in gem collection (12.2\% $\pm$ 3.2\% at offset $+0.40$). The precision of the technique is notably high in conv2a, likely due to it being the first layer where the next target is being established in the hierarchy as illustrated by Table~\ref{tab:probe_whole_layer}.

Conv1a, despite having minimal entity probe accuracy (20.0\% overall), shows strong steering capabilities with a different distribution than that observed in the other layers (detailed results in Appendix~\ref{app:additional_offset_results}). Unlike the overlapping, nuanced patterns in later layers, conv1a exhibits discrete zones: green dominates the negative range (60-73\% from -3.0 to -0.5), blue shows a sharp peak near the baseline (92\% at $+0.1$), and red controls the positive range (50-75\% from +0.5 to +3.0). Surprisingly we find the best gem steering in conv1a with 50\% $\pm$ 5\% gem collection at $+1.9$ offset, far exceeding conv2a (12\%) and conv4a (3\%). This suggests conv1a interventions are able to shift the normal bias towards collecting keys first, before target integration occurs. 

We also investigate whether the colours in the background have a significant impact on which entities are selected for targeting, and we find in an n=1000 sample that there is no statistically significant relationship between the colours of the background and the choice of entity (p=0.21). Figures for conv3a, conv2a, and conv1a, including more precise sweeps over smaller regions are included in Appendix~\ref{app:additional_offset_results}.

\subsection{Quantitative Patching Results}
\label{subsec:quantitative_steering}

We apply our incremental patching experiments across all channels in conv3a and conv4a and find a large number of channels which successfully redirect the agent away from a given entity. We present our results from layer conv4a in Figure~\ref{fig:steering-dot-plot}. For conv3a a similar phenomenon is observed but with far more channels and many more effective patching values. We add black dotted lines to demarcate the 99.9th percentile activation threshold. This demonstrates that for many channels, the effective patching values are far outside their normal activation range, indicating that the patching functions as a kind of superstimulus and is likely not part of normal functioning for the network. 

A possible explanation for why the ranges have an upper limit beyond which they stop working is that the activation is so strong that it disrupts normal functioning of the network, meaning that the agent fails to reach the artificially activated region.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{figures/entity_distribution_plot_focused_blue_key_gem_green_key_red_key_layer_conv4a_top_14_ood_p99.9_step_35001.pdf}
\caption[Conv4a activation patching results by entity]{Activation patching results for layer conv4a at checkpoint 35k, showing which activation values successfully redirect the agent away from each entity type. The x-axis shows the patched activation value applied to a single spatial location in each channel, while the y-axis shows the channel index. Each coloured dot indicates a successful intervention where the agent moved towards the patched location rather than the entity. Different entities (blue key, green key, red key, gem) show distinct effective value ranges, with some channels responding to multiple entities at different activation magnitudes. \label{fig:steering-dot-plot}

The fact that specific value ranges seemed to correspond with specific entities reinforces the idea that the magnitude of the activation plays a real role in specifying a particular entity.

The primary value of this finding is in providing further support for the way in which individual channels seem to multiplex the targeting of specific entities with varying activation strengths. We trained SAEs on these layers and found they exhibit similar multiplexing structures, suggesting this is a robust encoding strategy rather than simply the result of polysemanticity.

\section{Using SAEs to Validate Activation Encoding}
\label{sec:sae_training}
    \subsection{SAE Performance}
    \label{subsec:sae_performance}
    SAEs recovered 99.6\% (conv4a) and 102\% (conv3a) of task performance as measured by average reward, with the slight over-recovery likely due to random variance. Recovery occurred within 40,000 training steps, with variance explained scores approaching 100\% within 120,000 steps. SAEs were trained for 2 million steps to ensure convergence. This strong recovery indicates the SAEs worked as intended and could reliably validate our other results.


    \subsection{SAEs Preserve Base Model Structure}
    \label{subsec:sae_structure}
    Training SAEs on conv3a and conv4a did not reveal additional interpretable structure beyond what we observed in the base model. SAE latents exhibited the same patterns of systematic activation level differences between game states, with variations in the intervention spans. This preservation across radically different representation schemes suggests the activation patterns we observe are fundamental to the operations of the network and not simply an artifact of compression.

    Our primary method for using SAEs to ensure that we hadn't simply found polysemanticity in the network was to use the same quantitative patching approach described in Section~\ref{subsec:quantitative_steering} applied to all of the latents in an SAE. If it were natural to encode different entities into separate channels, we would expect the SAE to learn distinct latents for each entity type. Instead, we observe broadly the same patterns: individual SAE latents successfully redirect the agent away from multiple entities, with similar activation patterns to the base model. Figures illustrating this can be observed for conv3a and conv4a SAEs in Appendix~\ref{app:sae_interventions}.

\subsubsection{Patching with Dead Latents}
\label{subsec:dead_channel_steering}
Many effective steering latents were `dead latents' that exhibited zero activation during normal operation. We confirmed these remained inactive across 100 rollouts of typical maze-solving behaviour.

We attribute this to SAE training dynamics. These encoder-decoder pairs initially encoded useful signal during early training, but were subsequently suppressed as the L1 sparsity penalty forced lower activations. While encoder weights were driven toward near-zero values, decoder weights retained the directional structure learned when the latent was active. Artificially activating these dead latents during intervention experiments allows the preserved decoder weights to project meaningful directions into the activation space, influencing agent behaviour despite the latents never activating during normal operation.

\section{Exploring The Role of Negative Activations}
\label{sec:negative_activations}
While the activation strength encoding mechanism above is clear and seems connected to increasingly negative activations over the course of an episode, it is unclear what the negative activations are doing in the network and why the activations become less negative over time. 

To understand this, we analysed how negative activations changed in the convolutional layers over the course of a rollout. We observed that negative regions follow a clear pattern: regions previously explored in the maze have their activation values shift to near zero upon the collection of an entity, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:disinhibition_conv4a}. In this figure, channel 18 is chosen because it has the highest mean activation variance of all channels, clearly demonstrating the shifting effect. 

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.98\textwidth]{figures/disinhibition_paper_figure_v2.pdf}
    \caption[Progressive disinhibition in Conv4a during Heist gameplay]{Progressive disinhibition in Conv4a during Heist gameplay. Top row: Game observations with blue overlay indicating regions of strong negative pre-ReLU activation (intensity proportional to magnitude). Bottom row: Spatial activation maps for Conv4a channel 18, with mean activation ($\mu$) shown below each panel. At the start of an episode, activations are strongly negative across most spatial locations ($\mu = -12.6$). As the agent collects entities (keys, locks), these negative activations progressively release toward zero, with mean activation increasing to $\mu = -0.8$ by the end of the rollout. This pattern suggests the network uses negative activations to suppress representations of future objectives, with suppression lifted as each objective is completed.}
    \label{fig:disinhibition_conv4a}
\end{figure}


Collection triggers a phase shift: the entity's region becomes marked as empty, while regions of the maze still containing entities remaining largely negative. We observe this phenomenon across mazes and in layers conv2a, conv3a, and conv4a. This corresponds with the jumps in mean activation observed in Figure~\ref{fig:activation_tracking}, providing a clearer picture of what causes this phenomenon.

We test this mechanism by using patching in the convolutional layers to clamp activations in regions we do not want the agent to enter to a value of 0. This completely stops the agent from entering those regions. Tests in our cross maze environment (Figure~\ref{fig:spatial_steering}) reveal 100\% retargeting of the agent to regions that are not clamped.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.98\textwidth]{figures/steering_figure.pdf}
    \caption[Spatial steering via conv4a channel clamping]{Top row: baseline behaviour where the agent moves toward the blue key (left arm). Bottom row: after clamping activations to zero in three spatial regions (red dashed boxes), the agent instead moves toward the green key in the remaining unclamped region (green box).}
    \label{fig:spatial_steering}
\end{figure}

To verify that this mechanism operates spatially rather than on entity representations, we shuffled entity positions across maze arms and tested whether clamping still redirected the agent. With shuffled positions, clamping a region caused the agent to avoid that direction regardless of which entity occupied it, which we refer to as repulsion. Through backward elimination, we identified a minimal set of 13 channels sufficient for position-invariant spatial repulsion: [0, 2, 3, 9, 11, 12, 14, 15, 20, 26, 29, 30, 31]. This set reliably prevents the agent from entering any clamped region regardless of which arm of the maze the entity was in. Some channels were particularly important for repelling the agent such as channel 26, which lowered the successful repulsion rate by 25\%.

This causally demonstrates the spatial navigation mechanism: negative activations mark regions of interest, while near-zero activations indicate regions to avoid. This leads the model to navigate toward areas of negative activation. The entity retargeting we observe through activation offsets likely works because early stages of the game have more negative activations overall; shifting the offset mimics the activation patterns of later game stages rather than directly encoding entity identity.



\section{Probing for Entity Representations}
\label{sec:probe_results}

To determine where the network encodes information about which entity to target next, we trained linear probes on activations from each layer. We gathered 14,000 balanced samples across seven entity types (blue/green/red keys and locks, plus gem) and trained multi-class classifiers with a 90/10 train-test split. This approach reveals at which layer the network most explicitly represents the current goal.

\subsection{Convolutional Layers Encode Entities}

The probe accuracies across layers reveal that the convolutional layers dominate in encoding which entity to target, while the fully connected layers rapidly transition to translating information into actions. The probe accuracies illustrate two phases of information processing:

\textbf{Phase 1 - Emergence (conv1a $\rightarrow$ conv3a):} The whole layer probe of Conv1a performs barely above random baseline (20.0\% $\pm$ 2.1\% vs 14.3\%), on everything except the green key (99.4\%). However, examining individual channels (full results in Appendix~\ref{app:channel_probe_results}) reveals that conv1a contains highly specialised entity detectors: five channels achieve $>$90\% accuracy on blue keys (with channel 11 reaching 100\%), five channels exceed 90\% on red keys (channels 9 and 14 both at 100\%), and three channels on the gem (with channel 0 at 97.3\%). 
This shows that conv1a contains specialised entity detectors, but whole-layer entity information is not yet linearly separable at this early stage, suggesting integration occurs in later layers. In contrast, conv2a achieves 96.6\% $\pm$ 1.0\% accuracy across all entity types, representing a 76.6 percentage point jump. This dramatic difference shows that conv2a integrates entity information into a structure from the signals in conv1a. Performance improves slightly in conv3a (99.3\% $\pm$ 0.4\% accuracy) before showing a slight decline at conv4a (97.6\% $\pm$ 0.8\%).

\textbf{Phase 2 - Compression (fc1 $\rightarrow$ fc3):} The clarity of entity representation decreases progressively through the fully-connected layers, dropping from 87.5\% $\pm$ 1.7\% (fc1) to 76.8\% $\pm$ 2.2\% (fc2) and finally collapsing to 20.4\% $\pm$ 2.1\% in fc3, barely above random. To confirm whether the information was simply being represented non-linearly, we trained MLP probes (two hidden layers with ReLU activations) on these layers. The MLP probes performed very similarly to the linear probes, suggesting the information loss is genuine rather than a limitation of linear readout. 

This marks the point at which the network transforms explicit entity representations into action-oriented features. This makes sense as in final layer it has to produce only the action distribution and value estimation, which would naturally be quite distant from the entity representations themselves.

\label{subsec:directional_probes_results}

The directional probes reveal a significantly different structure than the next target entity probes. Conv2a achieves only 42.7\% $\pm$ 2.4\% accuracy (barely above the 25\% baseline), with highly unbalanced performance, achieving near-zero accuracy on downward movement (5.5\%) despite reasonable performance on horizontal directions. The best convolutional layer performance is observed at conv3a (63.6\% $\pm$ 2.4\%), before showing a somewhat surprising drop at conv4a to 51.7\% $\pm$ 2.5\%. 

Crucially, unlike entity information which degrades in fully-connected layers, directional information \textit{recovers and improves} through the FC layers. FC1 recovers to 62.9\% $\pm$ 2.4\%, and fc2 achieves the highest accuracy overall at 67.3\% $\pm$ 2.3\%, with balanced performance across all four directions. This suggests that spatial navigation information is actively refined and strengthened as the network prepares for action selection, rather than being compressed away.

The surprising conv4a performance drop (63.6\% $\rightarrow$ 51.7\%) diverges from the pattern observed in the next target entity probes, which retain most of their accuracy at this layer (99.3\% $\rightarrow$ 97.6\%). This suggests conv4a functions as a bridge in converting the high level spatial information into a structure coherent to the fully connected layers that temporarily disrupts the linear separability of directional information.

\subsection{Comparing Entity and Directional Information Flow}

The contrasting progressions reveal a two-stage processing architecture. Entity information contains specialist detectors even at conv1a and peaks at conv3a (99.3\% $\pm$ 0.4\%), while directional information is entirely absent at conv1a (25.1\% $\pm$ 2.1\%, at random baseline) and peaks much later at fc2 (67.3\% $\pm$ 2.3\%). This indicates that the network implements a hierarchical processing strategy:

\textbf{Stage 1 - Goal Identification (conv2a--conv4a):} Entity information emerges sharply from conv1a (which contains specialist entity detectors despite poor whole-layer performance) through conv2a (96.6\% $\pm$ 1.0\%) and peaks at conv3a (99.3\% $\pm$ 0.4\%), remaining extremely high at conv4a at 97.6\% $\pm$ 0.8\%. At this stage, directional information remains relatively weak (63.6\% $\pm$ 2.4\% at conv3a), suggesting the network prioritises identifying the correct goal before planning how to reach it. Notably, conv1a contains no directional information whatsoever (25.1\% $\pm$ 2.1\%, at random baseline), contrasting with its entity specialist channels, indicating that directional processing emerges later in the network.

\textbf{Stage 2 - Navigation Planning (fc1--fc2):} As entity information compresses (99.3\% $\rightarrow$ 20.4\%), directional information strengthens (63.6\% $\rightarrow$ 67.3\%). The network transforms explicit entity representations into spatial navigation commands, with peak directional accuracy at fc2 (67.3\% $\pm$ 2.3\%) suggesting motion-oriented features directly relevant to action selection. Both types of information then collapse at fc3 (entity: 20.4\% $\pm$ 2.1\%, direction: 21.1\% $\pm$ 2.0\%), as they are compressed into the final action distribution and value estimates.

This functional separation explains key findings from our intervention experiments. The near-perfect entity encoding at conv3a explains why patching and ablation interventions were most effective at this depth: we modified explicit goal representations before their transformation into motor commands. Conversely, the distributed nature of directional information across multiple layers explains why navigation remained largely functional even under heavy ablation, making it more robust to localised disruption than the concentrated entity representations.




\section{Ablation Studies}
\label{sec:ablation_results}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{figures/clean_heatmap_no_header.png}
    \caption[Single-channel ablation heatmap]{Heatmap showing entity collection counts when only a single channel is active (all others ablated). The vertical axis shows different entity types (gem, blue key, green key, red key), while the horizontal axis shows channel indices. Brighter colours (yellow) indicate higher collection success rates.}
    \label{fig:ablation-heatmap}
\end{figure}

Our ablation analysis reveals that the ability to navigate is surprisingly redundantly encoded throughout the network. The heatmap in Figure~\ref{fig:ablation-heatmap} shows the results in full. We observe that all channels have some success reaching entities, with the best performing channels achieving success rates greater than 40\% for certain entities, while others show lower than 10\% success rates. Some channels show similar performance across entities, while others are significantly better at particular entities.

We performed extensive targeted ablation experiments to better understand the functional roles of different channel groups, including ablating intervention spans (the activation ranges where patching successfully redirected the agent), navigation channels (those with highest independent collection rates), and infrastructure channels (those whose removal most impacted collection). However, these experiments yielded complex and often counterintuitive results that did not clearly illuminate the network's core mechanisms. Full results are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:ablation_studies}.

The most interpretable finding was that intervention spans alone are not sufficient to drive entity collection. When we preserved only the intervention spans for each entity while ablating everything else, performance fell below random-control baselines: 31.8\% for gem versus 44.1\% random, 61.8\% for blue key versus 74.9\% random, and similar patterns for other entities. This confirms that the intervention spans encode entity-relevant information but depend on other navigation circuitry to be effective; they represent one component of a distributed system rather than a self-contained targeting mechanism.






\section{Summary of Key Findings}
\label{sec:results_summary_key_findings}

Our investigation reveals four fundamental insights into how neural networks implement goal-directed behaviour in complex environments:

\textbf{1. Activation Patterns Track Game State.} Our heist network exhibits systematic changes in activation patterns over the course of a rollout, with mean activations shifting from strongly negative to near-zero as entities are collected. Parallel rollouts show consistent activation offsets between runs targeting different entities (correlation 0.931 in conv4a), and complete behavioural redirection is achievable through uniform offset adjustments, shifting from 93\% blue key collection at baseline to 94\% green key at +4.8 offset. This offset steering likely works by mimicking the activation patterns characteristic of different game stages rather than directly encoding entity identity. The network's strong baseline preference for the blue key, with occasional deviations to the green key, supports our hypothesis regarding a learned hierarchical structure.
This pattern persists across layers and even through SAE decomposition, suggesting it represents a fundamental computational strategy rather than an architectural artifact.

\textbf{2. Negative Activations Provide Spatial Gating.} The network uses negative activations to mark regions of interest, with near-zero activations indicating regions to avoid. As entities are collected, their associated regions shift from negative to near-zero, progressively disinhibiting the spatial representation (Figure~\ref{fig:disinhibition_conv4a}). Clamping activations to zero in any region completely prevents the agent from entering it, confirming the causal role of this gating mechanism. This spatial gating provides the foundation for the offset steering effect: early game stages have more negative activations overall, so shifting the offset mimics being at a different stage of the game.

\textbf{3. Two-Phase Processing Architecture Separates Goal Selection from Navigation.} Probe experiments reveal distinct information processing stages: entity determination occurs in convolutional layers (peaking at 99.3\% accuracy in conv3a) while navigation planning happens in fully-connected layers (peaking at 67.3\% directional accuracy in fc2). Conv4a serves as a critical bridge, maintaining entity information while reformatting spatial representations. This separation explains why patching interventions succeed at conv3a/4a: we intervene precisely where goals crystallise, before transformation into distributed action plans.

\textbf{4. Layer Depth Determines Intervention Character.} Despite minimal probe accuracy (20.0\%), conv1a shows strong but chaotic patching effects with discrete entity zones and uniquely achieves 50\% $\pm$ 5\% gem collection, far exceeding later layers. Conv2a offers the finest control with ±0.5 offset precision, representing the optimal intervention point where representations first integrate. Conv3a/4a provide stable but coarser manipulation of crystallised representations. This progression from raw detection through integration to crystallization defines both the network's computational strategy and our ability to control it.

These findings demonstrate that understanding where and how information is encoded, not just what is encoded, is crucial for interpreting and controlling neural network behaviour.
